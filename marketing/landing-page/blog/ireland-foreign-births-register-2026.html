Foreign Births Register 2026: Claim Irish Citizenship Through a Grandparent

No income test. No residency requirement. A €278 fee. If you can document the right ancestral chain, this is the cheapest EU passport on GoThere's entire catalog — but the ordering of who registers when can quietly disqualify your own kids.

What the Foreign Births Register actually is

Ireland's Foreign Births Register (FBR) isn't an application for citizenship — it's a registration of citizenship you may already hold under Irish law by descent. If you have an Irish-born parent, you're generally already an Irish citizen and registering on the FBR is how the state formally records it and issues you a passport. If your connection is a grandparent rather than a parent, the chain runs through that grandparent's child (your parent) — Ireland's Department of Foreign Affairs (DFA) sets out the exact multi-generational rules, and they matter, so treat this article as a starting point, not the final word.

Who typically qualifies

  • Anyone with a parent born in Ireland (direct FBR registration)
  • Anyone with a grandparent born in Ireland, where the Irish-citizen parent registers on the FBR first, then the grandchild can register in turn
  • No income requirement, no residency requirement, no language test

Because this is a citizenship-recognition process rather than an immigration application, the entire evaluation comes down to paperwork: birth, marriage, and death certificates connecting you to your Irish ancestor, in the right order, from the right jurisdictions.

The rule that catches families off guard

Timing matters more than people expect. A parent who registers on the FBR becomes an Irish citizen "by descent" from that point of registration — and children born after that registration don't automatically inherit citizenship the same straightforward way children born before it might, under some circumstances. In practice: if you're planning a family and want future children to qualify through you, register on the FBR before having more kids, not after. The DFA's own guidance covers the specific generational and sequencing rules in more depth than any blog post can responsibly summarize — read it directly before you plan around this.

Pros and cons

Pros

  • Full EU passport — live, work, and study anywhere in the EU
  • No residency requirement, ever, to keep it
  • By far the cheapest EU citizenship-by-descent route on our list

Cons

  • Registration order/timing affects whether your children qualify — get this wrong and you've created extra work for the next generation
  • Documentation standards are strict; missing or mismatched records (name spelling changes across generations, for example) cause real delays
  • DFA processing backlogs regularly stretch toward the long end of the 6–12 month window
