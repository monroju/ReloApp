Foreign Births Register 2026: Claim Irish Citizenship Through a Grandparent

No income test. No residency requirement. A €278 fee. If you can document the right ancestral chain, this is the cheapest EU passport on GoThere's entire catalog — but the ordering of who registers when can quietly disqualify your own kids.

Check your line in a minute: the free citizenship by descent checker runs your parent, grandparent or great-grandparent through the Foreign Births Register rules, including the registration-order trap.

What the Foreign Births Register actually is

Ireland's Foreign Births Register (FBR) is how someone born outside Ireland to an Irish citizen who was also born abroad becomes an Irish citizen. Which route applies depends on where your Irish ancestor sits in your family line:

  • Parent born in Ireland: you are already an Irish citizen from birth. You do not use the FBR; you apply for an Irish passport directly. One catch: if that parent was born on the island on or after 1 January 2005, they were only a citizen at birth if one of their own parents was Irish or British, or had lived there long enough.
  • Grandparent born in Ireland: this is the FBR route. You register yourself; your parent does not have to register first. A grandparent born in Northern Ireland counts the same.
  • Great-grandparent born in Ireland: only works if your parent was registered on the FBR before you were born (see the rule below).

No income requirement, no residency requirement, no language test. The whole assessment is paperwork: civil birth, marriage and death certificates connecting you to your Irish ancestor.

Sources: Department of Foreign Affairs, Registering a foreign birth; Citizens Information, Foreign Births Register. Last verified 26 October 2026.

Documents you will need

  • The printed application form, signed and witnessed by someone from the DFA's list of professions who knows you personally
  • Your original civil birth certificate showing your parents, a certified copy of your photo ID, two proofs of address and four passport photos
  • Your parent's original civil birth certificate and certified ID (or death certificate)
  • Your Irish-born grandparent's original civil birth certificate showing their parents, and their ID or death certificate. For Northern Ireland births this comes from GRONI. A printout of a free online record is not an original certificate
  • Marriage or name-change documents for anyone whose name changed

The rule that catches families off guard

Citizenship through the FBR starts on the day you are registered, not on the day you were born. So it passes to children born after your registration, and not to children born before it. If you qualify through a grandparent and plan to have children, register first.

This is also why the great-grandparent route usually fails: it needs your parent to have been registered before you were born. One exception: registrations made, or applications lodged, in 1986 or earlier count from the parent's birth, so the order does not matter for them (Irish Nationality and Citizenship Act 1956, s.7(3); 1994 Act, s.2).

Pros and cons

Pros

  • Full EU passport — live, work, and study anywhere in the EU
  • No residency requirement, ever, to keep it
  • By far the cheapest EU citizenship-by-descent route on our list

Cons

  • Registration order/timing affects whether your children qualify — get this wrong and you've created extra work for the next generation
  • Documentation standards are strict; missing or mismatched records (name spelling changes across generations, for example) cause real delays
  • Processing takes about a year, and nothing is decided until every original document is in
